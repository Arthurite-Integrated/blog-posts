Web3 was supposed to change everything.

No central authority. No single company controlling the system. No dependence on traditional intermediaries.

Just decentralized networks, blockchain technology, smart contracts, and applications that give users greater control over their digital assets.

But here’s an interesting question.

**If Web3 is supposed to be decentralized, why do so many Web3 applications still depend on centralized cloud infrastructure?**

Did we decentralize the application only to host half of it on someone else’s server? 😂

The answer is more interesting than you might think.

To understand it, we need to look beyond the blockchain and examine what actually happens behind the scenes when someone uses a Web3 application.

**First, What Exactly Is Web3?**

To understand Web3, it helps to look at how the internet has evolved.

**Web1** was largely about consuming information. Websites were mostly static, and users primarily read content.

**Web2** introduced interactive platforms, social media, cloud applications, and user-generated content. People could create, share, and interact, but much of the infrastructure and data remained controlled by centralized organizations.

**Web3** introduces the idea of building internet applications around decentralized networks, blockchain technology, digital ownership, and smart contracts.

Instead of relying entirely on a central database or organization to maintain records and enforce certain rules, applications can use blockchain networks where transactions are verified and recorded through distributed consensus.

For example, a traditional application might store ownership information in a company’s database.

A blockchain-based application could represent ownership through a token recorded on a public blockchain.

But here’s where things get interesting.

**Decentralizing ownership or transaction records doesn’t automatically decentralize every component of an application.**

And that’s where cloud computing enters the picture.

**You Built a Decentralized App. Now What?**

Imagine you’re a developer building a decentralized marketplace.

Users can connect their crypto wallets, purchase digital assets, and interact with smart contracts deployed on a blockchain network.

You’ve written your smart contracts.

You’ve built the frontend using React.

You’ve integrated wallet authentication.

Everything works perfectly on your laptop.

Congratulations!

But now you want thousands of people around the world to use your application.

Where will you host the frontend?

How will your application retrieve transaction histories efficiently?

Where will you store product images and other large files?

How will you monitor performance?

What happens when thousands of users open your application simultaneously?

Suddenly, you’re dealing with problems that traditional web applications have faced for years.

**And blockchain doesn’t automatically solve them.**

A decentralized application, commonly called a dApp, may rely on a blockchain for transactions and smart contract execution while using conventional infrastructure for other operations.

That infrastructure might include cloud servers, APIs, databases, storage systems, and content delivery networks.

In other words, your application can interact with a decentralized blockchain while still depending on centralized services to provide a smooth user experience.

**The Blockchain Is Not Your Entire Application**

One of the biggest misconceptions about Web3 is that everything happens directly on the blockchain.

In reality, blockchains are not designed to replace every component of traditional application infrastructure.

Storing large images, processing complex searches, serving web pages, or running conventional backend workloads directly on a blockchain can be impractical or expensive.

Consider a Web3 marketplace.

A smart contract might handle the ownership and transfer of a digital asset.

But the asset’s image, description, search functionality, analytics, and user interface may operate outside the blockchain.

This creates two important layers.

**On-chain components** handle operations recorded or executed on the blockchain, such as smart contract interactions, token transfers, and ownership records.

**Off-chain components** handle activities outside the blockchain, including frontend hosting, indexing, file storage, notifications, analytics, and supporting APIs.

The challenge is designing these layers to work together reliably.

And this is one reason cloud computing remains relevant to Web3.

**Where Does AWS Fit Into Web3?**

Amazon Web Services (AWS) provides infrastructure that can support many of the off-chain components used by blockchain applications.

Let’s return to our fictional decentralized marketplace.

**1. Running Blockchain Nodes With Amazon EC2**

A blockchain node is a computer that participates in a blockchain network by maintaining blockchain data, validating information according to the network’s rules, or providing access to blockchain state.

Applications often need to communicate with nodes to retrieve blockchain information or submit transactions.

Some developers use third-party node providers, while others operate their own infrastructure.

With **Amazon EC2**, an organization can provision virtual servers to run supported blockchain node software, configure networking, manage storage, and monitor resource usage.

However, running a node on AWS doesn’t automatically make that node decentralized.

The blockchain network may be decentralized, but the individual node is still hosted on infrastructure operated by a cloud provider.

That distinction matters when evaluating resilience and trust.

**2. Storing Files With Amazon S3**

Imagine your marketplace allows users to upload images, videos, or documents associated with digital assets.

Putting every large file directly on a blockchain would generally be inefficient and expensive.

Instead, applications often store files off-chain.

**Amazon S3** provides scalable object storage for files such as images, documents, and application assets.

Web3 applications may also use decentralized storage technologies such as the InterPlanetary File System (IPFS).

The difference is important.

S3 stores objects using cloud infrastructure managed by AWS, while IPFS uses content addressing and a peer-to-peer network for retrieving content.

However, storing a file through IPFS doesn’t automatically guarantee that it will remain available forever. Content must still be hosted or pinned by participating providers.

Some applications use a hybrid approach, combining conventional cloud storage with decentralized storage mechanisms based on their requirements.

The right choice depends on availability, cost, performance, ownership, and trust considerations.

**3. Processing Events With AWS Lambda**

Suppose someone purchases a digital asset through your marketplace.

A smart contract records the transaction on the blockchain.

But your application may still need to send a notification, update an off-chain database, generate a receipt, or refresh the user’s dashboard.

These tasks don’t necessarily belong on the blockchain.

**AWS Lambda** allows developers to run code in response to events without managing the underlying servers.

For example, an application could use a blockchain event listener to detect a confirmed transaction and invoke backend processing.

Lambda could then update supporting systems or trigger additional workflows.

The important detail is that the application must account for blockchain-specific behaviour, such as transaction confirmation requirements and possible chain reorganizations.

A transaction appearing on a network doesn’t always mean it has reached the level of finality your business process requires.

**4. Monitoring With Amazon CloudWatch**

A decentralized application can still experience performance problems.

Its API might become slow.

A blockchain node might fall behind the network.

An indexing service might stop processing events.

A supporting database might become unavailable.

Even if the blockchain continues operating normally, users may experience a broken application.

**Amazon CloudWatch** can help teams monitor the AWS-hosted components of their systems through metrics, logs, dashboards, and alarms.

Because decentralization doesn’t eliminate the need for monitoring.

**You still need to know when your infrastructure isn’t working.**

**So, Is Web3 Really Decentralized?**

Here’s where the conversation becomes interesting.

Imagine a Web3 application uses a public blockchain for smart contracts but relies on one cloud provider for its frontend, backend APIs, and blockchain access.

If that provider experiences an outage, users might temporarily lose access to the application.

The blockchain itself could remain fully operational.

The smart contracts could still exist.

The assets could still belong to their owners.

But the website might be unavailable.

Does that mean the blockchain is centralized?

Not necessarily.

It means **the application’s accessibility depends on infrastructure that may contain centralized points of failure.**

Decentralization isn’t always an all-or-nothing property.

An application can decentralize ownership and transaction verification while retaining centralized components for performance, convenience, or operational reasons.

What matters is understanding which parts are decentralized, which parts aren’t, and what happens when a dependency fails.

For example, could users interact with the smart contracts through another interface?

Could the application connect to alternative blockchain nodes?

Could its content be retrieved through another storage provider?

Those questions reveal more about an application’s actual resilience than simply calling it Web3.

**Cloud Computing and Web3 Aren’t Enemies**

At first glance, Web3 and cloud computing seem to represent opposing ideas.

Cloud computing often relies on infrastructure managed by large service providers.

Web3 emphasizes decentralization, distributed trust, and reducing dependence on individual intermediaries.

But they aren’t necessarily solving the same problem.

Blockchain technology can help establish shared records, execute smart contracts, and coordinate transactions without relying on one central authority to control the ledger.

Cloud computing provides flexible infrastructure for running applications, processing information, storing data, and delivering digital services.

One doesn’t automatically replace the other.

In many cases, they complement each other.

A Web3 application might use blockchain for ownership and settlement, AWS for application infrastructure, and decentralized storage for selected digital assets.

This is a **hybrid architecture**, combining centralized and decentralized technologies according to the needs of the application.

The goal shouldn’t be to decentralize everything simply because decentralization sounds innovative.

It should be to choose the architecture that actually solves the business problem.

**What Does This Mean for Businesses?**

For businesses exploring blockchain technology, the important question isn’t simply:

**“Should we use Web3?”**

It’s:

**“What problem are we trying to solve, and does blockchain offer a meaningful advantage?”**

Consider a company building a supply-chain tracking platform.

It might use blockchain to maintain a shared record of certain transactions between independent organizations.

But it could still use cloud infrastructure to host dashboards, process operational data, manage user access, and integrate with existing business systems.

Another business might build a digital asset marketplace that uses smart contracts for ownership transfers while relying on AWS infrastructure for its APIs, analytics, storage, and monitoring.

In both cases, blockchain is only one component of the overall solution.

And sometimes, a traditional database and conventional cloud architecture may be more appropriate than blockchain.

**Good engineering isn’t about using the newest technology everywhere. It’s about choosing the right technology for the problem.**

**Conclusion: Decentralization Still Needs Infrastructure**

Web3 introduces a different way of thinking about digital ownership, trust, and how applications interact with shared networks.

But it doesn’t eliminate the need for servers, storage, networking, APIs, monitoring, or reliable application delivery.

Some of those services can be decentralized.

Others may operate on conventional cloud infrastructure.

And many real-world applications combine both approaches.

The real challenge is understanding the trade-offs.

Where should trust be distributed?

Which components need to be decentralized?

Where does centralized infrastructure make sense?

And how can the entire system remain reliable when individual components fail?

Because at the end of the day, users don’t care whether your application runs on a blockchain, a cloud server, or a combination of both.

They care that it works.

**Web3 may change who controls the system. Cloud computing can still help keep the system running.**

And sometimes, the future of the internet isn’t about choosing between centralized and decentralized technologies.

**It’s about knowing when—and how—to use both.**
